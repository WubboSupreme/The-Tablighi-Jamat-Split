How to read a history in which the facts themselves became arguments

The Tablīgh split is unusually difficult to narrate because the central controversy is not only what happened, but what earlier events meant. The same 1995 announcement can be read as the abolition of a single supreme amīr, as a temporary emergency compromise among three responsible elders, or as a two-level system in which a world shūrā coexisted with rotating decision-makers at Nizāmuddīn.

Method. This dossier therefore uses four layers. First comes the event floor: dates, deaths, meetings, letters, named councils, court cases, police interventions and other matters independently or multiply attested. Second come the participant narratives: what Mawlānā Saʿd’s supporters, the ʿĀlamī Shūrā camp, and individual elders say those events meant. Third come retrospective motive narratives—“he waited for the elders to die,” “they engineered a coup,” “this was Gujarati factionalism,” “this was simply a fiqh of leadership dispute”—which are important historically because people acted on them, but which cannot be treated as proved merely because they are repeated. Fourth comes a source-critical assessment explaining where the documentation is strong, weak, late, or internally contradictory.

Scope warning: “comprehensive” here means every major documented narrative located across the academic literature, mainstream reporting, court material, elder letters, Shūrā publications, Nizāmuddīn-supporting Urdu/English publications and testimony reproduced by opponents. It does not elevate every WhatsApp rumor, anonymous accusation, or unverifiable social-media claim into a historical “version.” Where a serious factional source preserves such a claim, it is included and labeled.

Common ground

Hardest to dispute

Deaths, formal lists, public letters, the existence of meetings, the 2015 thirteen-name proposal, the 2016 physical rupture, later parallel institutions.

Contested

Meaning is the battlefield

Whether 1995 ended amīrship, whether 2015 was valid replenishment or an invalid new council, whether bayʿah implied a unilateral claim, and why senior elders left.

Source discipline

Names do not equal neutrality

An elder can be an eyewitness and still be a party. A factional website may preserve a genuine signed document while surrounding it with advocacy. Each item is treated at the level its evidence supports.

I • Before the schism

From Mewat to a global daʿwah movement

Mawlānā Muḥammad Ilyās Kāndhlawī (1885–1944), formed by the Deoband–Saharanpur scholarly world and alarmed by what he saw as the thin religious formation of many Muslims in Mewat, developed a practical program of lay religious renewal centered on movement, companionship, repetitive teaching, mosque-based life and face-to-face invitation. Historians commonly date the recognizable Tablīgh movement to the later 1920s, with Nizāmuddīn in Delhi becoming its spiritual and organizational heart. The name “Tablīghī Jamāʿat” itself became conventional; the movement preferred to present itself not as a party but as the work (mehnat/kām) of daʿwah and iṣlāḥ. S1 S2 S3

The succession precedent matters

Later arguments about 1995 constantly reached backward to 1944 and 1965. When Ilyās died in 1944, a consultative process selected his son Mawlānā Muḥammad Yūsuf Kāndhlawī as the second amīr. The point emphasized by anti-dynastic readings is that biological descent did not itself constitute appointment: the elders consulted. The point emphasized by Nizāmuddīn-oriented readings is the opposite but compatible fact: Tablīgh did in fact function for decades around a single recognized amīr, and consultation did not abolish amīrship. Both statements can be true at once.

Yūsuf’s period transformed an Indian revivalist effort into a transnational missionary network. After his sudden death in 1965, Mawlānā Inʿām al-Ḥasan Kāndhlawī was selected as the third amīr. Retrospective histories mention support among some Mewatis for Mawlānā Hārūn, Yūsuf’s son, but Hārūn accepted the elders’ decision. This episode later became a precedent in arguments against hereditary entitlement: a son of the previous amīr could be passed over by consultation. Nizāmuddīn supporters respond that this proves only that succession requires mashwarah—not that a permanent corporate shūrā must replace a single amīr. S4

II • Vocabulary that became constitutional law

Amīr, shūrā, faiṣal, mashwarah, zimmedār, bayʿah

Much of the split is unintelligible if English words such as “leader,” “chairman” and “committee” are treated as exact equivalents. Tablīgh developed a lived constitutional vocabulary whose terms overlap but are not identical.

Amīr / amārat

A recognized leader whose authority is expected to operate through consultation. Nizāmuddīn writers stress that an Islamic collective cannot be permanently headless. Shūrā writers reply that the effort can retain local amīrs and a rotating faiṣal without one lifetime world amīr.

Shūrā

A consultative body. The critical dispute is not “consultation versus no consultation”—both camps invoke shūrā—but whether a standing world shūrā is the final constitutional authority and can replenish itself.

Faiṣal

The person who concludes a particular mashwarah. In the 1995 controversy this is decisive: if the Nizāmuddīn elders were merely rotating faiṣals within a world shūrā, death did not transfer supreme authority to the survivor; if they were the recognized responsible leaders sharing amārat, survivorship carries more weight.

Zimmedār

Literally one carrying responsibility. Supporters of Saʿd sometimes use this language to soften the claim that he “self-appointed as amīr”; opponents answer that what mattered was the authority exercised, especially bayʿah and unilateral appointments.

Mashwarah

The actual consultative process. One of the deepest 2015 disputes is procedural: was Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb authorized as the deciding faiṣal after consultation, or did a group produce names after a meeting that had already ended without a decision?

Bayʿah

A pledge associated with spiritual and organizational allegiance. Shūrā sources make its suspension in 1995—and Saʿd’s alleged resumption of it after 2014—central evidence of a forbidden restoration of personal amīrship. Nizāmuddīn accounts challenge either the permanence, meaning, or jurisdiction of that prohibition.

Most important analytical point: the split is not adequately described as “one-man rule versus consultation.” Saʿd’s side also describes him as consulting a shūrā; the Shūrā side also accepts that a mashwarah has a deciding person. The deeper disagreement concerns where sovereignty in the work resides, who can constitute the highest council, and how authority survives the death of officeholders.

III • The institutional seed of the later dispute

1993: Inʿām al-Ḥasan and the ten-member shūrā

In the final years of Mawlānā Inʿām al-Ḥasan’s life, a ten-member senior body was formed to share responsibility for the expanding worldwide effort. Sources differ slightly over whether to date the decisive step to 1992 or 1993, but a cluster of elder letters places a major formation in 1993, with a preliminary discussion around the Hajj period and a formal list later that year. The fact of a ten-member body is common ground; its intended constitutional destiny after Inʿām al-Ḥasan’s death is not.

Two incompatible readings of Inʿām al-Ḥasan’s intention

Shūrā reading

The ten were not merely a pool from which a fourth amīr would be selected. They were a world-level collective designed to preserve the nahj of the work and prevent future concentration of authority. In this reading, the 1995 decision simply activated the collective structure Inʿām al-Ḥasan had already established. Later deaths therefore created vacancies to be refilled, not a ladder by which the last survivor inherited the whole movement. S5 S6

Nizāmuddīn reading

Inʿām al-Ḥasan’s shūrā assisted a living amīr; it did not prove that Tablīgh should permanently abolish amārat after him. Some Nizāmuddīn-aligned accounts explicitly analogize the ten to the group designated by ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb to choose a successor: the council existed in order that a leader might emerge. Even where no single leader was agreed in 1995, the compromise is read as provisional rather than a constitutional revolution. S7 S8

Why this matters: if the 1993 shūrā was meant to become a self-perpetuating world council, the 2015 Raiwind expansion looks like repair. If it was primarily an advisory/succession body under an amīr, the same act can look like the invention of a new sovereign institution. Neither camp’s conclusion follows merely from the word shūrā.

IV • The hinge of the entire controversy

June 1995: one meeting, several constitutions

Mawlānā Inʿām al-Ḥasan died on 10 June 1995. Senior figures gathered at Nizāmuddīn. They did not publicly install an undisputed fourth amīr. From that point onward, however, memory divides.

What is broadly shared

The surviving members of the ten-member body and other elders conducted several days of consultation. Three men became especially central in the Nizāmuddīn arrangement: Mawlānā Iẓhār al-Ḥasan, Mawlānā Zubayr al-Ḥasan and Mawlānā Muḥammad Saʿd. Miyājī Mihrāb is repeatedly described as a decisive senior figure in the consultation and as the person who publicly announced the arrangement. Soon afterward Iẓhār died, leaving Zubayr and Saʿd as the two most visible Nizāmuddīn leaders for roughly the next seventeen to eighteen years. S8 S9

Narrative A — a constitutional transfer to collective rule

Shūrā / elder-letter narrative

In this version, the elders considered appointing a single amīr but concluded that the responsibility of patronizing the work would rest with the existing global shūrā. Nizāmuddīn would be run by those world-shūrā members who resided there, with a rotating faiṣal. Bayʿah at Nizāmuddīn would stop. Later Shūrā histories identify five Nizāmuddīn residents associated with this rotating arrangement—Miyājī Mihrāb, Mawlānā ʿUmar Pālanpūrī, Mawlānā Iẓhār al-Ḥasan, Mawlānā Zubayr and Mawlānā Saʿd—while other accounts emphasize the three who emerged as the public face of the settlement. S5 S10

A frequently repeated recollection attributed to Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb makes Saʿd himself a principal advocate of avoiding a single appointment: if Zubayr were made amīr, Saʿd’s adherents might detach; if Saʿd were made amīr, Zubayr’s adherents might detach. Therefore, the recollection says, the work should proceed by mashwarah without bayʿah to a new supreme amīr. This recollection is central to the Shūrā charge that Saʿd later violated an arrangement he had once defended. S10

Narrative B — three recognized responsible leaders, rotating in amārat

Nizāmuddīn-supportive Urdu narrative

A detailed Urdu letter published in June 2016 remembers three constituencies in 1995: people from Gujarat and Aligarh favoring Zubayr, Mewatis favoring Saʿd, and elders from Kandhla/Saharanpur favoring Iẓhār. It names Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Muftī Zayn al-ʿĀbidīn, Mawlānā ʿUmar Pālanpūrī, Mawlānā Saʿīd Aḥmad Khān and Miyājī Mihrāb among those resolving the dispute. Mihrāb is said to have been made the deciding elder, after which the three— Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd—were declared the responsible persons, each taking a turn in leadership. The author concedes that some elders still believed one amīr was preferable, but says all accepted the compromise. When Iẓhār died, Zubayr and Saʿd continued; when Zubayr later died, Saʿd was the only one left carrying that inherited responsibility. S8

Narrative C — “for the time being”: a temporary settlement, not abolition of amārat

Wasīful Islām’s recollection

Wasīful Islām of Bangladesh—later aligned with Nizāmuddīn—recalled Miyājī Mihrāb reading a written announcement to the public that, for the time being, Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd would continue the work. He remembers Iẓhār initially making the decisions, and then Zubayr and Saʿd functioning together after Iẓhār’s death. Nizāmuddīn supporters use the provisional wording to argue that 1995 cannot be turned into an eternal ban on amīrship. The Shūrā publisher preserving his testimony inserts a rebuttal: Nizāmuddīn’s local decisions should not be confused with the authority of the global ten-member council, whose members continued to meet internationally. S9

Narrative D — a layered system: world shūrā above, rotating Nizāmuddīn faiṣals below

Academic reconstruction

An Indonesian academic study of the schism reconstructs the settlement as a compromise in which a world consultative body remained, while three representatives— Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd—served as rotating faiṣals at Nizāmuddīn. This reading explains why later memories can truthfully stress both the ten-member shūrā and the three central Nizāmuddīn figures without necessarily describing identical offices. It also shows why the later dispute became so intractable: the difference between “the men who decide at the original markaz” and “the sovereign world body” remained tolerable while the same elders overlapped both circles, but became explosive after deaths reduced the overlap. V • The quiet years that were not actually quiet

1995–2014: a long dual leadership and the accumulation of unresolved questions

For ordinary participants, the post-1995 period could look stable. The work expanded, enormous ijtemāʿs continued, and Zubayr and Saʿd appeared together at Nizāmuddīn for years. Precisely because the arrangement functioned without a clean written constitution recognized by everyone, however, the same years generated two opposite historical intuitions.

Continuity narrative

“Two elders ran it together for eighteen years”

This is perhaps the strongest experiential argument on the Nizāmuddīn side. If decisions “came from Nizāmuddīn,” if Zubayr and Saʿd jointly carried the work, and if no global collective routinely overruled them in the perception of workers, then the natural reading after Zubayr’s death is that the remaining recognized leader continued the same responsibility. Nizāmuddīn writers often portray later claims of a sovereign world shūrā as a retrospective re-description of a system that had never actually functioned that way in daily life. S9 S12

Corporate-continuity narrative

“The council shrank; authority did not mutate”

Shūrā writers agree that Zubayr and Saʿd were immensely influential but insist that the world shūrā continued to meet around international occasions and that Nizāmuddīn was never legally superior to it. The real administrative failure, on this view, was that vacancies were not filled as senior members died. By 2000, some accounts say, only Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Zubayr and Saʿd remained from the ten. Allowing attrition to continue created the later crisis. S6

The “waiting for elders to die” narrative

In its strongest polemical form, some anti-Saʿd accounts say he deliberately waited for older shūrā members to die, resisted replacing them, and then elevated himself by default. This narrative is historically important because it explains how many Shūrā supporters interpret the sequence of attrition. But the evidence has two different levels: the observable fact is that members died and were generally not replaced; the motive claim—that Saʿd had a long-term plan to benefit from this—is an inference made by opponents, not something demonstrated by a contemporary document. A source-critical history should therefore state the accusation without converting it into fact. S6 S13

Why 1996 becomes the “real beginning” in two opposite retrospective stories

Nizāmuddīn retrospective

“The campaign against Saʿd began almost immediately.”

Some pro-Saʿd histories date the effort to make him controversial to 1996, not 2014. They say influential workers repeatedly tried to create distance between Saʿd and Zubayr, but the two continued together for nearly two decades. In this narrative, the post-2014 crisis is the eventual success of a much older effort to prevent Saʿd’s authority from maturing naturally. This is a motive narrative: it is evidence for how supporters remember the years, not independent proof of a coordinated nineteen-year conspiracy. S7

Shūrā retrospective

“The consolidation began when senior checks disappeared.”

Anti-Saʿd histories also make 1996 symbolically important, but for the opposite reason. After Iẓhār’s death, they allege that Saʿd acquired greater control inside the markaz, including control over finances/treasury, while vacancies and institutional checks were left unrepaired. Later writers connect this to the attrition thesis. The underlying changes in personnel are real; claims that financial control was part of a deliberate path to a later takeover remain partisan allegations unless supported by independent accounts. S13

The symmetry is revealing: each camp reads the same long coexistence of Zubayr and Saʿd as proof against the other. Saʿd’s supporters ask why a supposed despot could cooperate with Zubayr for so many years if his sole aim was power. Opponents answer that the presence of Zubayr and other elders was precisely what constrained unilateral authority until deaths changed the balance.

The alleged 1999 unanimity agreement

Shūrā-aligned documentary compilations describe an agreement among the remaining senior members—often naming Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Saʿd, Zubayr, Muftī Zayn al-ʿĀbidīn and Hājī Afẓal—requiring unanimity before changes to the established uṣūl/nahj of the work. This later became a major exhibit against Saʿd: innovations in reading material or method were said to violate a rule he had accepted. Nizāmuddīn sources either dispute the interpretation, question the scope of such documents, or argue that normal reform within the original purposes of daʿwah cannot be frozen by a past administrative arrangement. S14

Muntakhab Aḥādīth: a book becomes a constitutional symbol

The dispute over Muntakhab Aḥādīth illustrates how a technical change can become a proxy for authority. Shūrā narratives say Saʿd promoted the work in a way that displaced or altered the established collective taʿlīm around Faḍāʾil-i Aʿmāl, and date contested changes to the early or mid-2000s. In the broader Shūrā charge-sheet, this sat beside other practices described as departures: daʿwah–taʿlīm–istiqbāl, restricting certain daʿwah activity to the mosque, and new styles of Qurʾān/ḥadīth explanation. S6 S14

The Nizāmuddīn counter-story is almost the inverse. Mawlānā Shāmīm Aḥmad’s account says the material behind Muntakhab came from Mawlānā Yūsuf, was recovered by Saʿd, taken to Raiwind and reviewed by scholars before publication. In this telling, Zubayr and other elders did not reject the book as an illegitimate invention; the later portrayal of it as proof of rebellion exaggerates a more nuanced methodological disagreement. S12

Historical significance: By the time Zubayr died, the split was therefore not born from a blank slate. Two constitutional memories had already formed: one centered on Nizāmuddīn and its surviving responsible elders, the other on the continuity of a world shūrā and a fixed inherited nahj. The death in 2014 removed the human bridge that had allowed both memories to coexist.

VI • The succession vacuum opens

18 March 2014: the death of Mawlānā Zubayr al-Ḥasan

Mawlānā Zubayr al-Ḥasan, son of Inʿām al-Ḥasan and one of the central Nizāmuddīn figures of the post-1995 era, died in March 2014. At the global level, Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb in Raiwind and Mawlānā Saʿd at Nizāmuddīn were now the two most consequential survivors associated with the original ten. At Nizāmuddīn, Saʿd was plainly the dominant surviving senior from the former rotating arrangement. The question was what that dominance legally meant. S15

Nizāmuddīn argument

Natural succession by surviving responsibility

No new seizure was necessary: Saʿd had already been one of the recognized senior decision-makers since 1995. Iẓhār died; then Zubayr died; Saʿd remained. Supporters describe his taking charge as continuity, not self-coronation. Some explicitly say the burden of amārat wa-qiyādat now rested on him alone. S8

Shūrā argument

Survivorship does not create an office

Saʿd surviving the other Nizāmuddīn faiṣals gave him prominence, not ownership of the world movement. If the 1995 system was collective, the proper response to deaths was to restore the shūrā. The later resumption of bayʿah and claims of general amīrship are therefore read as a unilateral constitutional change. S6 S10

Was there a formal declaration?

Anti-Saʿd sources often state that he “declared himself amīr” in 2014, sometimes identifying renewed bayʿah as the practical declaration. Nizāmuddīn sources often frame the same period more cautiously: he “took charge” at the original markaz after the other joint leader’s death. The neutral historical minimum is therefore narrower than either slogan: Saʿd emerged as the unrivaled senior authority inside Nizāmuddīn, while many elders outside or within the markaz disputed that this made him the sole world amīr.

A third reading: a genuine unresolved constitutional problem

Academic and journalistic treatments are useful precisely because they need not solve the religious legitimacy question. They describe a movement whose formal succession mechanism had become ambiguous after the death of members of a council that had never been replenished. From this perspective, both later camps were systematizing a practice that had relied on personal authority and overlapping networks more than on a codified constitution. The crisis became acute when personal overlap disappeared. S15 S16

VII • The private disagreement becomes audible

2015: demands for a defined shūrā, reported confrontations, and rival memories of tone

By 2015 senior workers were openly pressing for a settled governing arrangement. Shūrā-side sources say elders repeatedly asked Saʿd to form or accept a defined shūrā and to refrain from unilateral changes to the inherited method. Nizāmuddīn accounts reply that Saʿd did have consultation around him, that “shūrā” did not require the new sovereign structure opponents were demanding, and that some of the agitation was an organized attempt to constrain a legitimate amīr. S6 S12

The 23 August confrontation as reported by the Shūrā camp

A widely circulated transcript/audio is presented by Shūrā-aligned publishers as a Nizāmuddīn mashwarah in which Professor/Dr. Ṣanāʾullāh pressed Saʿd on why there was no nominated shūrā. The exchange, as transcribed, moves from a disagreement over what counts as a shūrā to Saʿd asserting “I am the Ameer,” telling an objector who did not accept this to “go to Jahannam,” and later insisting that he was amīr more generally. Opponents treat the audio as direct evidence that the issue was not invented at Raiwind later that year. S17

The contextual rebuttal preserved in the later Raiwind discussion

Saʿd’s own response, as preserved in a transcript of the November Raiwind meeting, does not simply concede the opponents’ framing. When the earlier wording is raised, he disputes how the encounter is being presented and asks why the provocation and preceding rude speech are omitted. In other words, even where an angry phrase is accepted as having been uttered, the Nizāmuddīn defense is that an emotionally charged reply under confrontation cannot by itself establish a lawful constitutional appointment or a premeditated coup. S18

Opponents’ inference

The audio reveals a preexisting personal claim to universal amīrship; Raiwind’s later shūrā was a response to that claim.

Supporters’ inference

An angry exchange was stripped of context and weaponized. Real leadership is determined by the 1995 arrangement and the living practice of Nizāmuddīn, not one quarrel.

What the source proves

There was a serious confrontation over authority before the November 2015 reorganization. It does not, by itself, settle the legal meaning of 1995 or the intent of every participant.

VIII • The decisive rupture

November 2015 at Raiwind: replenishing an old shūrā—or creating a new sovereign body?

This is the second hinge after 1995. Remarkably, some testimony from the two camps overlaps on the chaotic first stage of the meeting and diverges on what happened next.

The initial consultation

A transcript associated with the Raiwind proceedings has Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb’s position conveyed to the meeting: only two members of the old body were left, so names should be supplied from Nizāmuddīn, Raiwind and Kakrail. Saʿd objects to the proposed architecture. His reported position is not “there should never be consultation”; rather, he argues that a world-level shūrā should not be constituted there in the proposed manner and that relevant global names should be resolved from Nizāmuddīn. The exchange therefore exposes a jurisdictional dispute: who has the right to constitute the center? S18

Shūrā narrative: “completion,” not invention

The Shūrā case says the ten-member body of Inʿām al-Ḥasan had withered by death and was now being completed or replenished. Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb was the recognized faiṣal of the mashwarah; names were discussed by old workers from the principal centers; and the 13-member arrangement restored a collective institution that Saʿd had no unilateral right to veto. In this framing, 2015 is the moment at which one surviving member refused the repair of the very body to which he belonged. S19 S20

Nizāmuddīn narrative: the meeting ended without a faiṣalah

Wasīful Islām’s testimony says the contentious meeting ended without a decision. Later, he says, a paper with names was produced and he was told that Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb had selected them. His objection is procedural: no valid mashwarah had reached that result, so signatures afterward could not retroactively turn the paper into a collectively constituted world shūrā. Nizāmuddīn-oriented accounts develop this further, alleging that Saʿd was absent when names were finalized, that the elderly and ill Hājī was induced to sign on assurances of consensus, and that the list was then distributed globally as a fait accompli. S9 S7

Ṭāriq Jamīl’s intermediate but Shūrā-supportive recollection

Mawlānā Ṭāriq Jamīl’s later audio account is especially valuable because it partly explains why the two memories can coexist. He reportedly acknowledges that an initial noisy session produced no satisfactory conclusion. His account then says senior figures from the old centers subsequently prepared a written set of names, Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb approved them, further discussion occurred, Saʿd still refused, and Hājī then formally issued the decision. Thus the Nizāmuddīn statement “the first meeting ended without a decision” and the Shūrā statement “a later decision was validly made” are not logically contradictory; the dispute is whether that second-stage procedure possessed legitimate mashwarah authority. S21

The genuine-reasoning narrative. The 2015 record is important because it resists a cartoon of either side. Saʿd can be read as arguing from an established center: Nizāmuddīn had been the original world markaz and global arrangements should be settled there with him, rather than by a newly empowered Raiwind-centered body. His opponents can be read as arguing from institutional continuity: Nizāmuddīn could not become owner of a transnational movement merely because deaths had left one resident survivor. The schism became irreversible because each side regarded the other’s proposed cure as the constitutional disease itself.
IX • After Raiwind

December 2015: two rival centers of legitimacy harden

The failed settlement did not simply create two lists of names. It produced two constitutional orders, each claiming continuity with the pre-split movement.

The Nizāmuddīn-side council

Accounts from the period describe Saʿd arranging a Nizāmuddīn shūrā that included old figures alongside newer names. Lists reproduced in Shūrā literature commonly include Mawlānā Saʿd, Mawlānā Ibrāhīm Dewlā, Mawlānā Yaʿqūb, Mawlānā Aḥmad Lāṭ, Mawlānā Zuhayr al-Ḥasan, Mawlānā Yūsuf b. Saʿd, Mawlānā ʿAbd al-Sattār, Miyājī ʿAẓmat and Dr. ʿAbd al-ʿAlīm. The two camps interpret the same list differently. Saʿd’s supporters cite it against the claim that he rejected shūrā as such; opponents argue that a council whose membership is determined under a sole amīr is not equivalent to the independent world shūrā demanded in 2015. S22

Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb’s objections to some Nizāmuddīn choices

A December 2015 letter attributed to Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb and addressed to Saʿd and senior Nizāmuddīn elders objects to some proposed appointments, particularly on grounds of insufficient worldwide recognition or youth, and urges the Nizāmuddīn members included in the thirteen-person world shūrā to rotate as faiṣals. If authentic as reproduced—and it is treated as a core document by the Shūrā side—it demonstrates that the disagreement was already highly specific: it concerned who could appoint whom, at what level, and by which inherited procedure. S23

“Nizāmuddīn is the world markaz” versus “no markaz owns the world effort”

Nizāmuddīn principle

The work began and was historically directed from Banglawālī Masjid/Nizāmuddīn. The old system involved an international mashwarah centered around the original elders, not an autonomous parliament that could relocate supreme authority to Raiwind. In this view, separating Raiwind from Nizāmuddīn was the rupture.

World-Shūrā principle

Nizāmuddīn is historically first and spiritually prestigious, but it is one markaz within a global effort. Once Inʿām al-Ḥasan placed India, Pakistan and Bangladesh elders in a shared world body, no geography could confer personal sovereignty. In this view, Saʿd’s refusal of collective replenishment was the rupture.

Did Saʿd temporarily close or restrict the markaz after Raiwind?

Shūrā histories say that after returning from Raiwind Saʿd effectively suspended or sharply restricted local Delhi jamaʿat activity at Nizāmuddīn for several weeks, leaving the center unusually quiet except for foreign visitors. They interpret this as pressure against the new world-shūrā decision. This detail appears in partisan documentary histories but is not as independently corroborated as the existence of the Raiwind dispute itself; it is therefore best treated as a reported episode rather than a settled fact. S13

“They prevented the two surviving elders from meeting” versus “we repeatedly tried to reconcile them”

Nizāmuddīn-supportive histories add another procedural grievance: after 2015, they say, Saʿd was effectively blacklisted in Pakistan and intermediaries prevented a direct meeting with Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, allowing an inaccurate anti-Saʿd account to harden until Hājī’s death in 2018. Shūrā participant accounts tell the reverse story: repeated efforts were made to secure Saʿd’s participation, signature or acceptance, but he would not agree to the completed shūrā. These claims are difficult to reconcile because they concern access, intentions and failed private mediation rather than a single public document. S7 S21

The “amīr is required” theological reframing

Some Nizāmuddīn-oriented writings explicitly argue that a permanent system without a single amīr resembles a parliamentary structure alien to the prophetic model of collective action. They insist that consultation and amīrship are complements, not alternatives: the amīr consults and then decides. Shūrā supporters answer that they do not reject leadership; every jamaʿat and mashwarah has responsibility and a faiṣal, but Tablīgh’s global daʿwah network is not a caliphate requiring one lifetime sovereign. This is a real conceptual dispute, not merely a fight over personalities. S24

X • The physical rupture

19 June 2016 at Nizāmuddīn: three narratives of the night the elders left

The June 2016 violence is where organizational disagreement became bodily danger. The occurrence of a serious clash, injuries, police intervention and the departure of senior opponents from the markaz is well attested. Responsibility, intention and even the immediate trigger are fiercely disputed.

Narrative 1 • organized purge

Intimidation to consolidate control

Shūrā publications describe a group of roughly 100–150 Saʿd supporters entering or moving through the markaz, assaulting those associated with the opposing elders, vandalizing property, and creating conditions under which figures such as Mawlānā Ibrāhīm Dewlā, Mawlānā Yaʿqūb, Mawlānā Aḥmad Lāṭ and Mawlānā Zuhayr al-Ḥasan could no longer safely remain. Their strongest versions allege that the violence was not spontaneous but part of a purge. S25

Narrative 2 • a law-and-order action went wrong

Not a planned attack on elders

The same Shūrā compilation preserves a defense attributed to Saʿd’s side: the incident related to an attempt to deal with two people accused of wrongdoing concerning donations or property; a crowd then became uncontrollable. In other retellings Saʿd simply denies ordering violence. This version treats the later claim of a planned anti-Shūrā purge as retrospective politicization. S25

Narrative 3 • immediate quarrel escalates

Mainstream press minimum

Dawn later described a serious confrontation following an argument involving a prayer mat, reporting around fifteen injured and police intervention. That account does not validate the elaborate command-and-control claims of either faction, but it independently confirms that the markaz experienced violent disorder severe enough to accelerate the institutional break. S16

What happened after the clash is less disputed than why it happened

Senior elders who opposed Saʿd ceased living or operating normally from Nizāmuddīn. Mawlānā Ibrāhīm Dewlā later wrote that ugly incidents had damaged the sanctity and reputation of the work and that he himself had witnessed disturbing conduct. The Shūrā camp treats the exodus as proof that Nizāmuddīn had been forcibly captured. Nizāmuddīn supporters describe the departing elders as having chosen separation after an internal political struggle rather than having been lawfully expelled from a neutral institution. S26

Source-critical floor: It is historically responsible to say that serious violence occurred and that it helped drive senior anti-Saʿd figures out of Nizāmuddīn. It is not responsible, without stronger independent evidence, to present as settled fact either that Saʿd personally ordered a planned purge or that the entire event was merely an apolitical scuffle over alleged thieves. Both are factional causal narratives.

The Saudi mediation narrative

A Shūrā-published report says Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb sought mediation by respected Saudi-based elders, named as Shaykh Ghassān Zarīʿ and Shaykh Fāḍil Basyūnī. In this account Saʿd initially welcomed their intervention as divine help, but later rejected what he saw as interference or a process exceeding their role. For the Shūrā camp this demonstrates repeated failed mediation; for Nizāmuddīn supporters, such delegations could not override the rightful internal authority of the original markaz. The report is valuable as evidence that mediation was attempted, but its reconstruction of motive comes through a Shūrā-aligned publication. S27

XI • A separate controversy that became entangled with the first

Darul Uloom Deoband: doctrine, speech, and the danger of conflating two disputes

Late in 2016 Darul Uloom Deoband publicly expressed serious concern about statements attributed to Mawlānā Saʿd. The seminary’s objections dealt with interpretation of Qurʾān and ḥadīth, remarks concerning prophets, scholars and other branches of religious service, and what it regarded as unacceptable or idiosyncratic exposition. This was a major blow to Saʿd’s standing among Deobandi scholars and rapidly became ammunition in the organizational struggle. S28 S16

What Deoband did not decide

Deoband subsequently clarified that its religious reservations should not be read as a judgment in the internal administrative dispute between Saʿd and the rival Shūrā. In other words, “we object to these statements” is not identical to “the 2015 ʿĀlamī Shūrā is constitutionally legitimate.” That separation is one of the most important corrections to partisan histories on both sides. S29

The 2017 Deoband campus restriction

Deoband also restricted Tablīgh factional activity among its students. Contemporary reporting quoted the rector explaining that the institution was not opposing the Tablīgh effort as such; it feared students being pulled into clashes between the two camps and said mediation had failed. This administrative measure is sometimes cited as if Deoband “banned Tablīgh” or formally chose one faction. The contemporary explanation is narrower: prevent the internal dispute from entering the seminary. S16

The rujūʿ (retraction) dispute

Saʿd issued retractions/clarifications concerning some disputed remarks. Nizāmuddīn supporters argue that he showed adab toward Deoband, withdrew objectionable formulations, and that opponents continued circulating the original charge while suppressing or minimizing his rujūʿ. Some go further and portray the Deoband episode as having been exploited to turn an administrative quarrel into a religious excommunication campaign. S7

Deoband’s later position was more qualified. It indicated that a retraction concerning the Mūsā-related statement was in some sense satisfactory, but said its broader concern about Saʿd’s intellectual style and other statements remained. Thus neither slogan—“Deoband completely cleared him” nor “Deoband adjudicated him out of Tablīgh leadership”—accurately captures the seminary’s own distinction. S30

XII • A global movement becomes two global networks

2017 onward: the argument leaves Delhi and Raiwind

By 2017 the split could no longer be contained as an elite disagreement among South Asian elders. Local markazes and national shūrās were asked—sometimes formally, sometimes through visiting delegations and relationships—to decide which chain of authority they recognized. Because Tablīgh had deliberately avoided the bureaucratic legal structures of a conventional membership organization, the answer was often messy: a mosque trust could align one way, a circle of old workers another, and ordinary jamaʿats continue cooperating until a property or scheduling dispute forced the question.

The two emerging systems

Nizāmuddīn / Saʿd alignment

Recognizes Banglawālī Masjid at Nizāmuddīn as the central historic markaz and Mawlānā Saʿd as the principal amīr/responsible authority. It commonly describes the rival world shūrā as a later innovation, a body constituted without proper consensus, or a Pakistan-centered separation from the original center. Its self-understanding is continuity.

ʿĀlamī Shūrā alignment

Recognizes the collective world shūrā renewed at Raiwind in 2015 as the continuity of Inʿām al-Ḥasan’s ten-member institution. It commonly describes the Saʿd network as a breakaway personal-amīr faction that retained physical control of the old Nizāmuddīn premises. Its self-understanding is also continuity.

Mainstream reporting in 2018 already found parallel alignments in Britain and international tension around India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. An academic study published in 2019 likewise treats the principal organizational question as whether Saʿd would possess sole authority or the movement would be governed by a global shūrā, and describes the result as an international schism rather than a purely Indian quarrel. S16 S31

Ethnic and regional narratives

Participants sometimes explain the split through regional blocs—Mewatis, Gujaratis, Bengalis, Pakistanis, or networks associated with particular markazes. Dawn observed that some local conflicts visibly mapped onto ethnic networks in Britain and India. That is evidence that ethnicity helped structure mobilization; it is not evidence that ethnicity alone caused the succession dispute. Factional claims such as “Gujarati conspiracy,” “Pakistani takeover,” or “family dynasty” should therefore be treated as political sociology of the split—how adherents made sense of alliances—not as self-proving causal explanations. S16

XIII • Bangladesh as the most violent theater

Kakrail, Tongi and Bishwa Ijtema: factional division becomes a public-security problem

Bangladesh matters because Kakrail was one of the three great South Asian centers repeatedly invoked in the 1993–2015 constitutional story, and because the dispute there became unusually public. Wasīful Islām emerged as a prominent Saʿd-aligned figure, while Qārī/Mawlānā Zubayr Aḥmad became associated with the opposing Shūrā alignment. These Bangladeshi names must not be confused with the late Mawlānā Zubayr al-Ḥasan of Nizāmuddīn.

2018: Saʿd’s visit and government intervention

When Saʿd traveled to Bangladesh amid opposition from local ʿulamāʾ and the rival Tablīgh camp, the government mediated an arrangement under which he would not attend the Bishwa Ijtema and would leave after staying at Kakrail. Contemporary Bangladeshi reporting treated the controversy as part of the international leadership split rather than merely a local theological quarrel. S32

Later in 2018 factional scuffles and a major confrontation around the Tongi grounds left one person dead and hundreds injured according to Bangladeshi reports. Both camps blamed the other. Management of Kakrail and the staging of ijtemāʿ programs became subjects of negotiated or state-supervised arrangements. S33 S34

December 2024: the schism is still lethal

Fresh clashes around the Tongi ijtemāʿ field in December 2024 killed at least three people and injured hundreds, with each faction giving a contrary account of who initiated the violence. Saʿd-aligned representatives described an attack on their people; the Zubayr/Shūrā side accused the rival group of attempting to seize the grounds. The very existence of mutually incompatible first-person blame narratives more than eight years after the Nizāmuddīn rupture demonstrates that the schism had become locally institutionalized and intergenerational. S34

A subsequent Shūrā-aligned mobilization in Dhaka demanded government restrictions on the Saʿd faction. That demand is evidence of the depth of the conflict, not evidence that the allegations used to justify it were judicially established. S35

XIV • Britain

From spiritual allegiance to mosque governance and property law

Britain shows how a movement famous for informal personal networks can be forced into formal law. In late 2017 rival supporters fought over control and recognition at London’s Tablīgh center; police were repeatedly called and the site was temporarily closed. Contemporary reporting mapped different UK centers to different alignments, while also noting that allegiances could track ethnic and regional networks. S16

By 2023 an English High Court property case could describe the schism as an institutional fact: one trust was associated with the World Shūrā position and another with the Saʿd/Nizāmuddīn position. A court describing the factions is not endorsing either theology; its importance is evidentiary. The split had become sufficiently stable to shape trust governance, property control and legal identity. S36

Why Britain is analytically important: It disproves the idea that the conflict remained a private debate among a few Indian and Pakistani elders. Once local legal entities had to decide which leadership they recognized, abstract terms such as markaz, amīr and shūrā acquired concrete consequences for buildings, trustees, schedules and access.
XV • The narrative atlas

Every major explanatory narrative, laid out without choosing one for you

The following are not all equally well supported. They are separated so that the reader can see which claims concern documents, which concern constitutional interpretation, and which concern motive.

1 • Attrition / usurpation narrative

“He waited them out.”

Vacancies in the 1993 shūrā were not filled. As elders died, Saʿd’s relative power increased. After Zubayr died, he resumed or accepted bayʿah and behaved as sole amīr. The strongest polemical form says this attrition was intentional from early on. Evidence level: attrition and later authority are observable; long-term premeditated intent is not independently proven.

2 • Surviving-faiṣal continuity narrative

“Nothing was usurped; only one of the recognized three remained.”

1995 publicly established Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd as the responsible men at Nizāmuddīn. Iẓhār died; Zubayr later died. Saʿd did not invent a new claim in 2014; he inherited the undivided burden already shared for years. Evidence level: the three-man arrangement is well attested; whether survivorship conferred world amīrship is precisely the disputed constitutional inference.

3 • Restoration narrative

“Raiwind 2015 repaired Inʿām al-Ḥasan’s institution.”

The ten-member world shūrā had collapsed demographically. Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, as senior surviving member/faiṣal, completed it with representative elders from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Saʿd’s refusal was therefore refusal of the collective system he inherited.

4 • Paper-coup narrative

“The meeting ended; then a council appeared on paper.”

The main Raiwind mashwarah reached no decision. Names were finalized afterward without Saʿd’s agreement; Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb’s signature was obtained on an inaccurate picture of consensus. The “world shūrā” was therefore a new body, not the old one. Evidence level: Wasīful’s testimony directly supports the “no initial faiṣalah” point; allegations about deception of Hājī are partisan and harder to verify.

5 • Genuine constitutional ambiguity narrative

“Both sides inherited different pieces of an uncodified system.”

Pre-1995 Tablīgh mixed charismatic amīrship with shūrā. Post-1995 it mixed a world council with powerful resident decision-makers at Nizāmuddīn. As elders died, no universally accepted rule specified whether authority devolved to survivors or the council should replenish itself. 2014–15 forced a constitutional question the movement had survived by postponing.

6 • Original-markaz narrative

“The center cannot be manufactured elsewhere.”

Nizāmuddīn is where the work began and where its principal decisions historically emerged. International mashwarah is legitimate, but a transnational corporate shūrā assembled at Raiwind cannot displace the original markaz and its recognized senior leader.

7 • Globalization narrative

“A global work cannot belong to one building or family.”

Inʿām al-Ḥasan deliberately included India, Pakistan and Bangladesh in a world shūrā because the movement had outgrown one locality. Nizāmuddīn’s prestige cannot convert residence or lineage into supreme legal authority.

8 • One-amīr sharʿī narrative

“Shūrā advises; leadership must still terminate in an amīr.”

A permanent headless “parliament” is said to be inconsistent with the Islamic model of organized collective action. Saʿd can consult a shūrā and still be amīr; therefore “amīr versus shūrā” is a false binary.

9 • Rotating-faiṣal narrative

“Leadership exists without one lifetime world amīr.”

Every jamaʿat and mashwarah has a responsible person and the world shūrā can rotate its faiṣal. Tablīgh is a daʿwah effort, not a polity requiring a universal sovereign. The 1995 arrangement is evidence that the elders consciously chose this form.

10 • Nahj-preservation narrative

“The dispute was also about unauthorized methodological change.”

Muntakhab Aḥādīth, daʿwah–taʿlīm–istiqbāl and Saʿd’s particular explanations are presented as departures from a method that could be changed only through senior consensus. Governance matters because it determines who can alter the work.

11 • Reform / purification narrative

“He was correcting accumulated customs.”

Saʿd’s supporters portray some contested changes as an attempt to bring the work closer to Qurʾān, ḥadīth and the intentions of Mawlānā Yūsuf, against an entrenched clerical or organizational culture that had turned inherited procedure into untouchable custom. Muntakhab becomes restoration, not innovation.

12 • Tarbiyyah / temperament narrative

“The office requires formation he did not display.”

Opponents cite confrontational audio, reported harshness toward elders and controversial bayāns as evidence that Saʿd lacked the temperament expected of a Hazratjī-level leader. Caution: this is an evaluative participant judgment, not a neutral psychological assessment.

13 • Smear-and-provocation narrative

“Private anger was edited into a theory of character.”

Supporters say hostile elders or activists circulated decontextualized recordings, magnified slips, and ignored provocation, repentance and decades of service. The conflict’s media ecosystem selected the most inflammatory fragments to delegitimize a leader already targeted administratively.

14 • Dynastic narrative

“The Kandhlawi family became a quasi-dynasty.”

Critics point to Saʿd’s descent from Ilyās and Yūsuf and later prominence of his son as signs that family prestige converted into office. Supporters answer that Saʿd was formally placed among senior decision-makers in 1993/95 and that earlier successors were themselves chosen by mashwarah, so lineage alone does not explain authority. Best use: lineage is a source of charisma, not a sufficient causal explanation.

15 • Regional / ethnic-bloc narrative

“Gujarat, Mewat, Bengal and Pakistan became political constituencies.”

Both factional histories name regional followings; journalists observed ethnic patterning in later local disputes. But the existence of blocs does not prove a master ethnic conspiracy. Regional networks are better understood as channels through which a constitutional dispute mobilized.

16 • Money / institutional-control narrative

“The real struggle was over assets and control.”

Some polemical sources make treasury access, donations, markaz control or property central. These issues certainly become consequential once factions separate, but claims of financial motive are especially difficult to substantiate and should not be used as a monocausal explanation without records.

17 • 2016 purge narrative

“Violence completed the takeover.”

The June clash is read as the point at which dissenting elders were physically driven from Nizāmuddīn, converting a disputed leadership claim into territorial control.

18 • Separation-by-opponents narrative

“The elders chose to leave after losing an internal dispute.”

In this counter-reading, Saʿd did not create a new faction; those refusing the historic center’s leadership organized elsewhere and then labeled Nizāmuddīn the breakaway. Violence is denied as a legitimate basis for rewriting that underlying sequence.

19 • Deoband-confirmation narrative

“The ʿulamāʾ later confirmed deeper problems.”

Shūrā supporters use Deoband’s criticism to argue that governance concerns were accompanied by substantive scholarly concerns about Saʿd’s religious speech.

20 • Deoband-separate-issue narrative

“A doctrinal correction cannot decide who owns the shūrā question.”

Nizāmuddīn supporters emphasize Saʿd’s rujūʿ and Deoband’s own administrative neutrality. Even if a scholar errs in a bayān, they argue, that does not validate a rival 2015 constitutional procedure.

21 • Quietist / “continue the work” narrative

“The factional argument is itself the fitnah.”

A substantial current of ordinary workers and some elders resists turning either constitutional theory into the center of religious life. Its language is practical: stop consuming WhatsApp polemics, keep the five aʿmāl, go in jamāʿat, preserve relationships and allow senior people to answer for their own decisions. This is not a solution to the succession question, but it is an important third response to the split and helps explain why many local workers avoided formal alignment for as long as possible. S12 S16

22 • Modernizer-versus-conservative narrative

“The constitutional fight concealed a struggle over what Tablīgh should become.”

Some journalists and Saʿd supporters describe him as challenging customs that had become rigid and attempting to re-anchor the effort in Qurʾān and Sunnah; critics describe those same moves as unauthorized alteration of a carefully preserved method. This narrative is useful for understanding why disputes over books, mosque procedure and bayān style became inseparable from governance, but “modernizer” and “conservative” remain analytical labels, not neutral verdicts. S16

23 • Zuhayr-rivalry narrative

“A demand for office by Zubayr’s son helped drive the rupture.”

A 2016 pro-Saʿd Urdu letter directly accuses Mawlānā Zuhayr al-Ḥasan and his supporters of pressing for him to become a faiṣal and for a shūrā system that would limit Saʿd. On this telling, what opponents call institutional reform was partly succession rivalry within the wider family network. Shūrā sources reject reducing the movement’s senior elders to Zuhayr’s personal ambition and point to non-family figures—ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Ibrāhīm Dewlā, Yaʿqūb, Aḥmad Lāṭ and others—who supported the collective structure. S8 S19

24 • Hājī-as-authorized-faiṣal narrative

“The process was noisy, but the deciding elder had authority to conclude it.”

This is the cleanest Shūrā response to Wasīful Islām. A mashwarah need not end with unanimous vocal agreement; its authorized faiṣal hears views and concludes. If Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb held that role, the later written decision was not a paper coup but the completion of the consultation. The Nizāmuddīn response is that the scope of his mandate did not extend to inventing a world-sovereign body after the meeting failed. S18 S21

25 • “Rujūʿ was suppressed” narrative

“The charge sheet remained public after the correction.”

Pro-Saʿd writers say controversial older statements were revived in 2016, that Saʿd retracted objectionable wording orally and in writing out of respect for Deoband, and that opponents continued to circulate the accusation without equal circulation of the retraction. Deoband’s own later clarification complicates the story: it acknowledged movement on a specific issue but said broader reservations remained. S7 S30

26 • “No constitution until there was a crisis” narrative

“Personal trust had been doing the work of law.”

The most institutional reading is that Tablīgh’s deliberate informality was a strength during expansion and a weakness during succession. Terms such as amīr, faiṣal and shūrā worked because the same elders trusted one another; the movement never needed to specify, in corporate-law precision, how a world shūrā replaces members, whether a markaz outranks it, or whether the last surviving faiṣal inherits general authority. Once trust collapsed, both sides reached backward and constitutionalized past practice differently.

XVI • What participants say was said

Reported dialogues: reconstructed carefully, not treated as stenographic certainty

The split has an oral archive: audios, recollections, translated transcripts and speeches. These are often more revealing than later manifestos, but also more vulnerable to clipping, translation choices and retrospective framing. What follows preserves the structure of the exchanges while avoiding invented precision.

A. The August 2015 Nizāmuddīn confrontation

Shūrā-hosted transcript / audio claim

Professor Ṣanāʾullāh challenges the lack of a formally nominated shūrā and asks, in substance, whether the work belongs to one individual or to a consultative collective.

Saʿd replies that a shūrā exists and pushes back against the assumption that only a fixed named body counts as consultation.

Other elders reject that answer and press the need for a defined council.

Saʿd, in the hostile transcript, says: “I am the Ameer,” and at one point tells an objector to “go to Jahannam.” The transcript later attributes to him a stronger universal formulation of amīrship.

How it is used: opponents treat this as a self-appointment caught on audio. Saʿd’s later response emphasizes confrontation and provocation, challenging the fairness of presenting the outburst as a free-standing constitutional declaration. S17 S18

B. Raiwind, November 2015: where should world authority be constituted?

Meeting transcript

Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb’s side, through those speaking for him, argues that the original world council has dwindled to two and needs additional members proposed from the main centers.

Saʿd objects to constituting the proposed sovereign shūrā in this form. His reported view is that Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb already bears responsibility at Raiwind, while matters of a world shūrā should be settled from Nizāmuddīn rather than established over it.

A participant brings up the earlier “I am amīr” confrontation as evidence of why a defined shūrā is necessary.

Saʿd disputes the presentation, saying in substance that rude treatment and the words that provoked his reply are being omitted.

Historical value: this exchange is powerful precisely because it shows Saʿd arguing about institutional jurisdiction rather than simply rejecting mashwarah. It also shows opponents understanding his behavior as a personal-amīr claim before the new shūrā was finalized. S18

C. Wasīful Islām after the first Raiwind meeting

Nizāmuddīn-aligned testimony, preserved by opponent

Wasīful Islām recalls being shown a paper with the new names after what he believed had been an inconclusive meeting. He objects: the mashwarah had ended without a decision, so how had a shūrā suddenly been made?

Those presenting the paper tell him, in substance, that Shaykh/Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb had chosen the names.

Wasīful treats this as the fatal procedural defect: one elder’s post-meeting selection is not equivalent to a valid global mashwarah.

The Shūrā publisher’s rebuttal is that Hājī was the authorized faiṣal and that the appointment did arise from the broader consultative process. S9

D. Ṭāriq Jamīl’s account of the same procedural gap

Participant recollection

Ṭāriq Jamīl says, in substance, that the first noisy deliberation did not solve the matter. Senior figures then worked through a written set of names; Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb approved; attempts were made to bring Saʿd along; Saʿd did not accept; and Hājī ultimately issued the arrangement.

This is one of the most useful pieces in the entire dossier because it narrows the disagreement. The sides need not disagree that a first session failed. They disagree about whether the later written procedure was a valid continuation of mashwarah or an unauthorized act after it. S21

E. The 1995 announcement in competing memory

Wasīful / Nizāmuddīn memory

Miyājī Mihrāb publicly announces that, provisionally, Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd will continue the work. Iẓhār functions as the senior deciding figure; after his death the two younger elders continue together. The accent falls on named persons and lived continuity.

ʿAbd al-Wahhāb / Shūrā memory

The elders decide against installing one new supreme amīr, preserve collective shūrā responsibility, arrange rotating decision-making and stop bayʿah. The accent falls on the constitution surrounding the named persons.

A useful way to hold both memories at once: an announcement can name the people who will operate the markaz while a wider agreement specifies the body within which they operate. The historical dispute is whether that wider agreement was meant to be permanent and world-sovereign. That is why merely proving that “three names were announced” or that “a shūrā existed” does not by itself resolve the schism.

XVIII • Master chronology

A century of Tablīgh in one scroll

1885

Birth of Muḥammad Ilyās Kāndhlawī

The future founder is born into the North Indian scholarly-religious milieu from which the movement will emerge.

1920s

Mewat experiments become a movement

Itinerant daʿwah, mosque-based teaching and personal reform coalesce into the recognizable Tablīgh method.

1944

Ilyās dies; Yūsuf is selected

First succession combines consultation with a single amīr.

1965

Yūsuf dies; Inʿām al-Ḥasan becomes third amīr

A minority preference for Yūsuf’s son Hārūn does not prevail; the movement continues under Inʿām.

1980s–early 1990s

Global scale raises governance problems

Later elder letters locate methodological disagreements and the need for wider consultation in this era, though these are often retrospective factional reconstructions.

1993

Ten-member world/senior shūrā formed

Inʿām al-Ḥasan gathers senior representatives from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh into a body whose posthumous purpose will later be disputed.

10–12 Jun 1995

Inʿām dies; succession mashwarah

No universally accepted fourth amīr is publicly installed. Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd become central at Nizāmuddīn; Shūrā sources emphasize collective world governance and cessation of bayʿah.

1996–97

Iẓhār al-Ḥasan dies

The three-man Nizāmuddīn arrangement contracts to the long Zubayr–Saʿd pairing.

Late 1990s–2000

The original ten shrink

Deaths leave fewer original shūrā members. Shūrā narratives later identify failure to replenish vacancies as a structural mistake.

1999

Agreement on changes to the method, according to Shūrā documents

Later compilations say surviving elders agreed that major changes required broad/unanimous consent. The scope and constitutional force are contested.

2002–06

Muntakhab Aḥādīth and methodological tensions

Opponents say Saʿd introduced changes without required consent; supporters say he revived Mawlānā Yūsuf’s own material and re-centered Qurʾān and ḥadīth.

18 Mar 2014

Zubayr al-Ḥasan dies

Saʿd becomes the dominant surviving Nizāmuddīn authority. Whether this equals world amīrship becomes the core constitutional question.

2014–15

Bayʿah and sole-amīr allegations intensify

Shūrā sources say Saʿd resumed personal bayʿah in violation of 1995; Nizāmuddīn sources frame his authority as continuity from the earlier arrangement.

23 Aug 2015

Reported Nizāmuddīn confrontation

An audio/transcript of a heated dispute over a defined shūrā circulates, later becoming a central exhibit in the anti-Saʿd narrative.

15–16 Nov 2015

Raiwind world-shūrā crisis

An initially contentious process is followed by a thirteen-member list. Shūrā supporters call it completion of Inʿām’s body; Nizāmuddīn supporters call its procedure invalid.

Dec 2015

Parallel governance hardens

Nizāmuddīn has its own defined council around Saʿd; Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb pushes a different arrangement based on the thirteen-person world shūrā.

19 Jun 2016

Violence at Nizāmuddīn

Injuries and police intervention are independently reported. Opponents call it a purge; Saʿd-side defenses deny orchestration or describe a different immediate trigger. Senior anti-Saʿd elders leave.

Aug–Nov 2016

Elder letters crystallize the Shūrā case

Ibrāhīm Dewlā and others circulate accounts; Raiwind meetings produce further explanatory documents and demands.

Dec 2016

Darul Uloom Deoband issues doctrinal criticism

A separate theological controversy becomes fused in public perception with the administrative split.

2017

Global national chapters divide

UK centers and other local networks publicly align with Nizāmuddīn or the World Shūrā; London clashes require police intervention.

2017–18

Deoband clarifies administrative neutrality

The seminary reiterates its doctrinal concern while distinguishing it from the question of which internal faction is organizationally legitimate.

Jan 2018

Bangladesh bars Saʿd from Bishwa Ijtema participation

Government mediation illustrates the dispute’s transformation into a public-order issue.

Dec 2018

Deadly Tongi confrontation

One person is reported killed and hundreds injured in factional violence.

2019

Academic literature names a full organizational schism

Studies begin analyzing how members in different countries respond to the rival leadership systems.

2020

COVID-19 crisis at Nizāmuddīn

The pandemic and state action place the Nizāmuddīn markaz under extraordinary pressure. This episode is historically major but should not be retroactively treated as a cause of the 2015–16 split.

2023

English court records the schism as institutional reality

A property/trust dispute describes distinct World-Shūrā and Saʿd-aligned networks in Britain.

Dec 2024

Three killed in renewed Bangladesh clashes

Competing blame narratives around Tongi show the split remains deeply institutionalized.

2025–26

No universal reconciliation

Both leadership networks continue to publish their own histories, operate aligned centers and describe themselves as the continuation of the pre-split Tablīgh effort.

How much weight should each kind of source carry?

Highest for narrow facts

Signed letters, dated lists, court judgments, contemporary government arrangements, and independently reported deaths/clashes are strongest for proving that an event or document existed.

Strong for synthesis

Academic and mainstream journalistic sources are strongest for mapping the dispute without adopting either side’s entire constitutional theory, though they can still contain errors or rely on interested informants.

Essential but interested

Factional sites and elder memoirs are indispensable for reconstructing what each side believes happened. They are weakest when moving from witnessed event to hidden motive, sweeping moral verdict or conspiracy.

XX • The narrowest defensible synthesis

What can actually be concluded?

The split did not begin because one day a united movement suddenly chose between “Saʿd” and “shūrā.” It emerged from a succession system whose different layers had been held together by shared elders, personal trust and ambiguity.

The movement’s first three eras accustomed workers to a single amīr chosen with consultation. Inʿām al-Ḥasan then created a transnational ten-member senior shūrā before his death. When he died in 1995, the elders did not agree on a universally recognized fourth amīr. They instead produced an arrangement in which named Nizāmuddīn leaders—especially Iẓhār, Zubayr and Saʿd—operated alongside a broader shūrā. That hybrid arrangement worked as long as the same prestigious men overlapped the institutions.

As those men died and vacancies were not replenished, two constitutional logics separated. One logic said: the recognized responsibility at the original markaz has narrowed to the remaining elder; consultation continues around him, and the normal Islamic form remains amīr-with-shūrā. The other said: the office belongs to the collective body created by Inʿām al-Ḥasan; death creates vacancies, not hereditary or survivorship sovereignty, and the shūrā must be restored.

Zubayr’s death in 2014 made the contradiction unavoidable. The confrontations of 2015 show that senior workers were already fighting about a defined shūrā and Saʿd’s authority before the Raiwind list existed. The November 2015 meeting then produced a procedural dispute layered onto the constitutional one: the Shūrā camp regards the thirteen-member body as a valid completion authorized by Hājī ʿAbd al-Wahhāb; the Nizāmuddīn camp regards it as a post-meeting construction lacking proper consensus. Both sides therefore identify the other as the innovator.

The June 2016 violence transformed an elite succession disagreement into territorial separation. Deoband’s later doctrinal criticism of Saʿd intensified the breach but did not create it, and Deoband itself distinguished its theological concerns from the internal governance question. By 2017 the two chains of authority were reproducing themselves in Britain, Bangladesh and elsewhere; by 2023 courts were encountering the divide in property governance; by 2024 people were still dying in factional clashes in Bangladesh.

The most useful final distinction is therefore between three questions that polemical histories often collapse:

1. Historical fact: what meetings, deaths, documents, clashes and appointments occurred?
2. Constitutional interpretation: what did the 1993 and 1995 arrangements legally mean for future leadership?
3. Moral motive: did Saʿd plan a usurpation, did opponents plan a coup, did jealousy, lineage, money, ethnicity or personal ambition drive events?

The first can often be established. The second can be reconstructed as rival coherent readings. The third is where certainty falls most sharply, and where responsible history must resist turning factional suspicion into fact.

That is also why the most accurate history is not a compromise narrative in which “both sides are equally right.” The evidence is uneven issue by issue. On some points one side has the stronger document; on others the opponent preserves a plausible procedural objection; on still others there is only retrospective attribution of motive. The historian’s task is to keep those evidentiary levels visible rather than flattening them into a verdict.
